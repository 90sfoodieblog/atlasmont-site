Business Geography: The National

The business counts enterprise: the firms Black Americans own, where those firms are, and the receipts they record. The Census Bureau's business surveys divide the territory in two. Employer firms — businesses with paid staff — number about 160,000 under Black ownership in recent Annual Business Survey readings, with combined receipts above $200 billion. Nonemployer firms — the sole proprietorships and independent operators without payroll — number about three million, an order of magnitude larger, and form the broad base of Black enterprise. This maps both tiers at national scale; the State section gives every state a business of its own.

Where the firms are

The geography of Black business follows the geography of Black population with instructive deviations. Texas, Georgia, and Florida lead in counts of Black-owned firms, as they lead in population, with New York and California close behind; metropolitan Atlanta records one of the densest concentrations of Black employer firms in the country, and the Washington, Houston, and Dallas metros read high on both counts and receipts. By share of a state's own business population, the ranking tilts further south and further metropolitan. Receipts concentrate more sharply than counts: a small fraction of firms — the largest employers — account for a large share of the $200 billion, a concentration the thematic business draw explicitly, because the count map and the money map are different maps and the atlas keeps them separate.

The ownership pattern

Two patterns inside the ownership data recur on every business in this atlas. The first is the role of Black women, who own roughly half or more of Black-owned employer firms and a still larger share of the nonemployer tier — the largest ownership share of any demographic pairing the Census Bureau publishes, and one that has grown across recent survey years. The second is sector shape: Black-owned employer firms concentrate in health care and social assistance, professional and administrative services, transportation, and food service, with construction and retail heavily represented in the nonemployer tier; the state business carry each state's sector table from the Annual Business Survey, so the national shape can be compared against every state's own. New-firm formation — the flow rather than the stock — is tracked in the Census Bureau's Business Formation Statistics, and the movement-oriented business use it to show where entry is running fastest.

How to read a business

Business statistics arrive with longer lags than any other series in this atlas: the Annual Business Survey's detailed state tables trail the reference year, and the name the vintage on every figure rather than smoothing over the delay. Definitions matter as much — the surveys classify a firm by the majority owner's demographics as the owner reports them, and firms with equally shared ownership sit in their own category, which the preserve rather than redistribute. Receipts are nominal dollars of the survey year. Within those stated frames, the business geography is one of the most legible in the atlas: it follows the population, concentrates in the metros, leans on services, and is carried to an unusual degree by women owners — and the fifty-one state business that follow in this atlas's largest section let a reader test that sentence against any state in the union.

Business inside the atlas

The business measure returns in the atlas at every scale, because enterprise is one of the ways a place's population becomes a place's economy. The forty metro economy rank the concentrations a national reader most needs — Atlanta's density of Black employer firms, the Washington metro's receipts, the Texas metros' growth in formations — and the gazetteer gathers every business reading for a place onto its single page. The thematic business separate what the headline counts join: employer from nonemployer firms, receipts from counts, the sectors from one another, and women-owned firms into their own full reading, since no account of Black business geography is honest without that share standing on its own line. Formation statistics add the time dimension, showing entry running strongest in the southern metros and in a set of smaller, fast-growing markets. The national work is to hold the frame steady — about 160,000 employer firms, about three million without employees, receipts above $200 billion, all of it following the population with a metropolitan tilt — so that the deeper can complicate it state by state without losing the reader.
