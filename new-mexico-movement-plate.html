Movement and Change: Black New Mexico

Populations move, and the census counts the result. This plate reads the movement record of Black New Mexico — the growth, the arrivals, and the departures written into the published counts for the state, whose Black population stands at 75,000, alone or in combination, 2 percent of the state on the alone count.

Growth and Migration

Black New Mexico begins in uniform: the Black regular regiments — the 9th and 10th Cavalry and the Black infantry regiments — garrisoned the territory's forts (Stanton, Bayard, Union, Selden) through the Apache campaigns and the border era, and their veterans seeded the territory's first Black communities in the fort towns and in Las Cruces (whose Black district dates to this era) and Albuquerque's Barelas and South Broadway neighborhoods. The settlers' chapter arrived with the homestead era: Blackdom, founded in 1903 in Chaves County south of Roswell by Frank Boyer and Ella Louise Boyer, grew into New Mexico's only all-Black incorporated town — a farming community of several hundred at its peak with its own school, church, and oil-era hopes — before the aquifer's limits and the Depression dispersed it; its site is marked and its story central to the state's Black heritage record. Smaller Black farming colonies and the railroad towns of Raton, Tucumcari, and Clovis complete the frontier layer.

The composition is disproportionately military and federal in origin — the veteran share of Black New Mexico's adults runs well above national figures in the survey data — layered over the homestead descendants and a growing African immigrant community in Albuquerque. Indicators present the small-population caution with a federal-economy cushion: incomes in the survey data run near the national Black figures (the laboratories and bases paying federal-scale wages), attainment lifted by the university and lab orbit, homeownership comparatively strong in the military cities. Health outcomes track the state's overall position in the public health tables. The state has no HBCU; the University of New Mexico's Africana studies tradition and the community's churches and associations carry the institutional layer.

The estimates show slow growth tracking the state's — Albuquerque and Las Cruces gaining, the plains towns' remnants shrinking — with the Black share holding near its modern level. The border-and-bases economy is the stability: New Mexico's Black population is the West's most federally anchored in percentage terms, and its data move with appropriations more than with markets.

The National Movement Around the State

In New Mexico, black movement in the present era runs in several directions at once: out of the largest older metros, toward the South and its suburbs, and into mid-sized cities whose Black populations are growing fastest in percentage terms. New Mexico, in the West, sits inside that wider current. The decennial counts and the survey estimates between them are the instruments that register the change; each new release revises the picture at the margin, and the direction of the lines — which places gain, which hold, which thin — is the story this plate is built to tell for New Mexico.

In New Mexico, movement is measured three ways in the federal record, and the plate distinguishes them. In New Mexico, natural increase adds births and subtracts deaths inside the state. What separates the two definitions is the multiracial population — real, growing, and small against shares of the size recorded in New Mexico. In New Mexico, international migration adds residents born abroad, a stream that has reshaped the Black populations of the largest gateway metros and, increasingly, of states well beyond them. For New Mexico, the published components separate the streams, and the balance among them is the state's movement signature.

For New Mexico, the national movement figures are the current; the state's own distribution, tabled below, is where the current lands. New Mexico holds about 75,000 Black residents — Black alone or in combination — roughly one in twenty-eight of its people, with Black alone at about 44,000, or 2.1 percent. It is in the rows below — New Mexico's own — that the general material becomes a specific place.

The migration currents read locally in New Mexico, where the state rows below set the population whose movement the national pattern frames. Albuquerque holds roughly half of Black New Mexico: the city is about 3 to 4 percent Black (some 18,000 to 20,000 residents), centered historically in the South Broadway and Martineztown districts and now spread through the Northeast Heights and the west side, employed by Kirtland Air Force Base, the national laboratories' service economy, the university, and the state government. The military south holds the second layer: Alamogordo (Holloman AFB) and Clovis (Cannon AFB) record Black shares in the high single digits on their bases' workforces; Las Cruces, the second city, is roughly 3 to 4 percent Black with New Mexico State's community; Roswell records a small share with the Blackdom legacy in its county; Santa Fe's Black population is small and professional in composition. Only as it sharpens the local record does the general one matter here — and the local record is New Mexico's, in the table.
