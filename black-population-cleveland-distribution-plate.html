Black Cleveland: Nearly Half of the City on the Lake

This metro plate opens the Atlasmont file on Black Cleveland: the population itself — its size, its share of the metro, and its distribution across the city and suburbs that make up the metropolitan whole.

In Cleveland, that pattern is the plate's whole subject: the table below gives the published share and count, and the map shades the share. Cleveland holds about 185,000 Black residents — Black alone or in combination, essentially half of the city's 373,000 people — and about 360,000 across the metropolitan area. Cleveland was one of the Great Migration's first destinations and among its most complete transformations: a city that was roughly 2 percent Black in 1910 became nearly half Black a century later, as its total population halved from its industrial peak. In Cleveland, held against the national currents, the state's own rows say how its movement stands.

The East Side Metropolis

The distribution reads at state scale in Cleveland, where the table's rows give the published count and the share the map draws. The migration-era community formed along Central Avenue and in Cedar-Central, then spread east through Hough, Glenville, Fairfax, Mount Pleasant, Lee-Harvard, and Buckeye-Shaker — a continuous Black urban landscape of brick apartment blocks, single-family streets, and commercial corridors, anchored by institutions like Karamu House (founded in the 1910s, the nation's oldest Black theater company) and a dense church geography. That is what a benchmark is for in an atlas: not to rank Cleveland, but to locate it.

Economy and Indicators

The distribution reads at state scale in Cleveland, where the table's rows give the published count and the share the map draws. Cleveland's Black poverty rate runs above 30 percent in the survey data — among the highest of the large cities in this section — and Black median household income trails the metro's other large-city figures, the arithmetic of a city whose manufacturing base collapsed and whose remaining employment concentrates in health care, education, and services. The benchmark's work is location, not rank: it fixes where Cleveland stands so the plate can be read.

Composition and Change

Black Cleveland is overwhelmingly native-born and Great Migration-descended, its families' southern roots in Alabama, Mississippi, and Georgia visible in the birthplace data of its elders; smaller Caribbean and African communities have layered into the east side and its suburbs in recent decades. The political data point (kept to demography here) is structural: a city half Black elects from a Black-majority electorate in most districts, and its Black institutional class — in the hospitals, the universities, the county government — is the region's most established. What the data cannot show is a growth engine: Cleveland is the profile of a mature Black metropolis managing contraction with depth.

Reading the Cleveland Numbers

Cleveland completes the industrial belt sequence of this section — Detroit, Chicago, Milwaukee's peers in pattern. Its lesson is the suburbs: nowhere else did Black suburbanization happen so early or so completely, and the contrast between East Cleveland's fiscal data and Warrensville Heights's stability, ten minutes apart at the same Black share, is the section's cleanest proof that demography alone determines nothing about a community's economics.

Cuyahoga County's full Black population, city and suburbs together, approaches 400,000 in the county tables.
