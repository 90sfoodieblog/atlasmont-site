The National Movement Around the State

In Louisiana, black movement in the present era runs in several directions at once: out of the largest older metros, toward the South and its suburbs, and into mid-sized cities whose Black populations are growing fastest in percentage terms. Louisiana, in the South, sits inside that wider current. The decennial counts and the survey estimates between them are the instruments that register the change; each new release revises the picture at the margin, and the direction of the lines — which places gain, which hold, which thin — is the story this plate is built to tell for Louisiana.

In Louisiana, movement is measured three ways in the federal record, and the plate distinguishes them. In Louisiana, natural increase adds births and subtracts deaths inside the state. What separates the two definitions is the multiracial population — real, growing, and small against shares of the size recorded in Louisiana. In Louisiana, international migration adds residents born abroad, a stream that has reshaped the Black populations of the largest gateway metros and, increasingly, of states well beyond them. For Louisiana, the published components separate the streams, and the balance among them is the state's movement signature.

Reading This Plate

In Louisiana, read the table from the top: the share first, because the share is what the map shades; then the count, because the count is what the share is made of. Shares and counts answer different questions. The share says how present Black life is in the everyday population of Louisiana; the count says how much of Black America lives there. Both are published figures, rounded as the source agency publishes them, and both are the ground every other Louisiana plate in this atlas stands on.
