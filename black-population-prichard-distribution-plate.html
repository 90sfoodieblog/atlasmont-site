Black Population in Prichard, Alabama

Prichard is a city of about 19,300 people in Mobile County, Alabama, directly adjoining the city of Mobile on its northwest — and about 90 percent of its residents are Black, a share matched by only a handful of American municipalities. Prichard began as a settlement of Black and working-class families outside Mobile's city limits, grew with the shipyards and wartime industry of the port, and incorporated as a separate city whose population has been overwhelmingly Black for generations. Its neighborhoods — including the historic Africatown-adjacent Plateau community at its edge — carry some of the deepest Black historical roots on the Gulf Coast. This article profiles Prichard's Black population in the census record: its size, its origins beside the port of Mobile, and its economic circumstances.

Size and Share

The 2020 census counted roughly 17,400 Black residents in Prichard, about nine in ten of the city's people. A share of 90 percent puts Prichard in the most concentrated tier of Black settlement in urban America, alongside a short list of inner-ring communities — East St. Louis, Illinois and a few others — where a Black population that formed under segregation-era boundaries has remained the city's defining demographic fact. Prichard's total population has fallen substantially from its mid-twentieth-century peak, when the city held more than 40,000 residents, but the decline has been proportionally heavier among its small non-Black population, so the Black share has risen even as the city shrank. Mobile County as a whole is about 35 percent Black; Prichard's concentration within it is the product of a century of housing patterns that steered Black families to this side of the municipal line.

Plateau, the Port, and a City of Their Own

Prichard's oldest Black community predates the city itself. In 1860 the last known slave ship to reach the United States, the Clotilda, landed its captives in the Mobile River delta, and the survivors — after emancipation — founded Africatown on the plateau just north of Mobile, a self-governing community of African-born founders whose descendants remain in the Plateau neighborhood at Prichard's southern edge. Around that nucleus, and around the rail yards and wartime shipbuilding that boomed in the 1940s, Black Mobile grew northward. When Prichard incorporated and expanded, it became the political home of that population: a Black-governed city in the era when Black political control of a Southern municipality was still rare. The city's institutions — its churches along the old corridors, its schools, Vigor High School's storied athletic tradition — grew from that foundation, and Prichard's identity as a Black city is a matter of deliberate civic inheritance, not accident.

Income and the Cost of Disinvestment

The census's economic figures record the strain of a small city carrying a big population's needs on a narrow tax base. Prichard's median household income is about $32,000, roughly 29 percent of residents live in poverty, and about 11 percent of adults hold a bachelor's degree or higher. The city has passed through well-documented fiscal crises, including a period in which its pension obligations overwhelmed its budget — a fiscal story that analysts cite as a case study in what happens when a shrinking, low-income tax base meets fixed obligations. Employment for Prichard's residents lies largely outside its borders: in Mobile's port and shipbuilding economy — Austal's shipyard and the Alabama State Docks among the region's anchor employers — its hospitals, and its service sector. The profile that emerges is of a community with an unmatched depth of Black history on the Gulf Coast, whose 90 percent Black population sustains a full civic life while working inside one of the tighter economic envelopes of any city in this reference. The geography of Black Mobile completes the frame. Across the county — in Mobile's own northern neighborhoods, in Plateau and Africatown, in Prichard and Chickasaw's older streets — the Black population of the metropolitan area forms a continuous community for which Prichard is the most concentrated expression: the one jurisdiction where that community is nine-tenths of the whole and governs itself outright. The 2020s brought new attention to that inheritance, as the discovery of the Clotilda's wreck in the Mobile River and the growth of Africatown's heritage institutions drew national focus to the plateau at Prichard's edge. The census numbers — 19,300 people, 90 percent Black — measure a community whose significance in American history is only now being fully reckoned.
