Black Kansas City: East of Troost

This metro plate opens the Atlasmont file on Black Kansas City: the population itself — its size, its share of the metro, and its distribution across the city and suburbs that make up the metropolitan whole.

In Kansas City, for Kansas, the pattern is not background but subject; the state's own rows, tabled below, carry it. Kansas City, Missouri, holds about 150,000 Black residents — Black alone or in combination, roughly three in ten of its 510,000 people — and the bi-state metropolitan area about 270,000. 18th and Vine: The Historic Center

In Kansas City, in Kansas, that pattern is the plate's whole subject: the table below gives the published share and count, and the map shades the share. The community's historic heart is the 18th and Vine district, east of downtown: the center of Black Kansas City from the early twentieth century, when the city's Black population — drawn by the stockyards, railroads, and packinghouses — was confined by covenant and custom to the east side. The museums now housed at 18th and Vine — the American Jazz Museum and the Negro Leagues Baseball Museum — institutionalize that record. Where the general meets the particular is the table of Kansas City; read the two together.

Troost: Reading the Line in the Data

The subject here is Black Kansas City: East of Troost, and the reading is the atlas's standard one: the published figures first, the interpretation after. The line's origin in the early twentieth century's development and lending practices is documented in the housing literature, and its persistence is the metro's central demographic fact: Kansas City's overall Black share of 30 percent is, in truth, two cities' figures — an east side at 70 to 90 percent Black and a remainder in the low teens — averaged by a street. In Kansas City, the figures are the argument; the text only keeps them in order.

The Metro: Two States, Several Black Geographies

The distribution reads at state scale in Kansas, where the table's rows give the published count and the share the map draws. Kansas City, Kansas (Wyandotte County) holds a Black population of roughly a fifth to a quarter of its 165,000 people, with its own historic Black community and the Strawberry Hill and Quindaro districts' deep history — Quindaro was a free-port town of the Underground Railroad era. Independence, Raytown, and Grandview on the Missouri side suburbanized the Black population from the 1970s onward; Grandview and Raytown are now substantially Black suburbs. The general record matters here only as far as it sharpens the local one, which Kansas City's table states plainly.

Economy and Institutions

The employment base for Black Kansas City runs through health care (the hospital systems on both sides of the line), the federal presence (the region is a significant federal employment center), logistics along the intermodal corridors, and the public sector. Lincoln University's reach, the University of Missouri-Kansas City, and the Metropolitan Community College system carry the education data; the city has no HBCU but sits within Missouri's Lincoln University orbit. Black median household income in the metro runs mid-pack in this section's Midwestern set, with the east side's tracts and the suburbs' medians separated by the widest intra-metro spread of any city in the region — the Troost arithmetic again, expressed in dollars.

Reading the Kansas City Numbers

Kansas City is the profile that teaches the reader to distrust a citywide average: no figure quoted for the whole city describes either side of Troost. Its Black population — 150,000 in the city, 270,000 in the metro — holds one of Black America's great cultural legacies and one of its sharpest internal divides, and the honest unit of analysis here is the side of the street.

A note on this plate's figures, stated once and plainly: Black residents of Kansas City, Missouri, approximately: 150,000; the street the data use as the metro's dividing line — the east side overwhelmingly Black, the west side overwhelmingly not: Troost Avenue.
