The Vermont Education Plate

In Vermont, education plates turn on attainment — the credentials a population carries. This plate places Black Vermont in the national education geography: the benchmarks below are the published national attainment figures for Black adults, and Vermont's distribution figures — 15,000 Black residents, 1 percent of the state — give the scale of the community those national patterns describe within the state's borders.

The National Pattern

The attainment ladder reads locally in Vermont: the state rows below give the population whose schooling this national pattern frames. For Black adults aged 25 and over, the Census Bureau's surveys record a population that has reached near-parity in high school completion while remaining well behind national rates in college completion: about 89 percent hold a high school diploma or higher, and about 25 percent — one in four — hold a bachelor's degree or higher. A benchmark in an atlas does not rank Vermont; it locates it, and that is its whole use here.

In Vermont, that national attainment pattern meets a state school and college system of its own, and the table below fixes the population it describes. At the first step, high school completion, Black adults stand at about 89 percent, close to the national figure of about 90 percent — a convergence that is historically recent and remarkable, given that as late as the mid-twentieth century a majority of Black adults had not completed high school. At the third step, the bachelor's degree, the Black figure of about 25 percent separates from the national figure of about 36 percent and the non-Hispanic White figure of about 39 percent. Reading This Plate

How to read this plate: the state rows are the published census figures for Vermont; the benchmark rows are the published national attainment record, labeled as benchmarks. In Vermont, attainment is a stock built over decades, so the national pattern changes slowly — and the size of the state's Black adult population is what turns that pattern into local classrooms, colleges, and workforces.

The Population Behind the Plate

The attainment ladder reads locally in Vermont: the state rows below give the population whose schooling this national pattern frames. Vermont holds about 15,000 Black residents — Black alone or in combination — roughly one in forty of its people, with Black alone at about 9,500, or 1.5 percent. It is one of the smallest Black populations in this reference, and it contains one of the largest historical facts in the education data: the first bachelor's degree earned by a Black American was awarded in Vermont, in 1823. The benchmark's work is location, not rank: it fixes where Vermont stands so the plate can be read.

The attainment ladder reads locally in Vermont: the state rows below give the population whose schooling this national pattern frames. Alexander Twilight — Middlebury College class of 1823, schoolmaster, minister, and the first Black American to serve in a state legislature (Vermont's, from 1836) — built the Old Stone House in Brownington that now anchors the state's Black heritage trail. Vermont's constitutional abolition of adult slavery (1777, the first of the American states) framed the free community's entire history — a fact the state's Black heritage institutions document, alongside the complications (child indenture persisted; the Underground Railroad ran through the farm country) the honest record includes. Not to rank Vermont but to locate it — that, in an atlas, is the use of a benchmark.

In Vermont, attainment is cumulative: every graduating class adds to it, and nothing subtracts except time. That is why education plates reward patience — the national Black attainment record has risen across every measured generation, and a state like Vermont, holding 15,000 Black residents, holds its share of that rising stock in its own schools and campuses.
