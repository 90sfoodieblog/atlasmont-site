The Black Population of Idaho

About 32,000 Black Idaho residents — one in fifty-seven — between Boise's historic River Street and a refugee hub's new generation. The census profile.

Distribution Across the State

Boise's Black community formed in the 1890s in River Street, the district between the Boise River and the railroad tracks southwest of downtown — a working neighborhood of porters, cooks, barbers, laborers, and their churches (St. Paul Baptist dates to the era) that remained the community's home for three-quarters of a century. The district's fabric was largely taken by urban renewal and freeway construction in the 1960s and 1970s, and the Erma Hayman House — a stone house built by a Black stonemason in 1907, preserved today as a city cultural site — stands as the neighborhood's surviving emblem and the anchor of Boise's Black heritage interpretation. Smaller historic communities existed in the railroad and mining towns (Pocatello's rail yards, the Wood River valley's service economy around Sun Valley, whose Black staff lived in Ketchum's segregated quarters in the resort's early decades) and around Mountain Home's air base after the Second World War.

In Idaho, the education data register the change first, as they do in every resettlement profile: the Boise and West Ada districts' Black enrollments have multiplied within a decade, English-learner programs in the valley serve large African cohorts, and Boise State's and the College of Western Idaho's Black student counts — small in share, growing in number — are the higher-education layer the next decade's attainment tables will be built from.

The State on the National

On the national, Idaho sits in the lightest band on the, under 6 percent. Its Black alone share of 1 percent — 47 of the fifty-one geographies mapped — stands against a national figure of 13.5 percent, and its Black population, 32,000 alone or in combination, is one of the building blocks of the national total of 52.8 million. An atlas reads Idaho two ways at once: by the size of its community, and by that community's weight inside the whole population. Idaho is legible on both readings, and this section of the atlas holds the two side by side.

Where the general meets the particular is the table of Idaho; read the two together. In Idaho's band — the lightest band on the, under 6 percent — the shaded peers include Alaska, Arizona, Colorado and Hawaii. In Idaho, banding is a cartographer's compromise: it trades precision for pattern, letting the eye take in the geography of a whole country at once. The table restores the precision the shading rounds away, carrying the published share and count for Idaho exactly as the source agency releases them.

In Idaho, every figure on this descends from the federal count of the whole population — the decennial census and the American Community Survey that updates it between census years. Those instruments count everyone, which is what makes a share meaningful: 1 percent is not a sample of Black Idaho, it is Black Idaho measured against all of Idaho. In Idaho, the atlas's other return to this population again and again, because income, schooling, health, and ownership are all attributes of the people counted here, in this place, first.

Idaho's profile is two communities sharing one small set of tables: the River Street descendants, whose neighborhood survives in one stone house and a city's memory work, and the newcomers of the resettlement generation, already the majority of the whole. The 32,000 in these counts are the sum of Idaho's Black past and its fastest-arriving future.

Reading This

In Idaho, read the table from the top: the share first, because the share is what the map shades; then the count, because the count is what the share is made of. Shares and counts answer different questions. The share says how present Black life is in the everyday population of Idaho; the count says how much of Black America lives there. Both are published figures, rounded as the source agency publishes them, and both are the ground every other Idaho in this atlas stands on.

A note on the two counts this atlas uses throughout. In Idaho, the Black alone count holds residents who marked Black and no other race; the alone-or-in-combination count adds residents who marked Black alongside another race. In Idaho, the second is the larger and the more inclusive, and the gap between them is itself a fact about the place — widest where multiracial identification is most common, narrowest where it is rare. For Idaho, the alone share of 1 percent drives the shading, while the combination count of 32,000 carries the community's full enumerated size.
