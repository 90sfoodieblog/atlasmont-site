The Tennessee Health Plate

In Tennessee, health plates turn on coverage and prevalence — who is insured, and what conditions a population carries. This plate places Black Tennessee in the national health geography: the benchmarks below are the published national figures for Black Americans, and Tennessee's distribution figures — 1.25 million Black residents, 17 percent of the state — give the scale of the community those national patterns describe within the state's borders.

The National Pattern

The coverage figures take their local form in Tennessee, where the state rows below set the population behind the national pattern. About one in ten Black Americans — roughly 9 to 10 percent in the Census Bureau's annual coverage report — has no health insurance at all, a rate above the national figure and above the White rate. Not to rank Tennessee but to locate it — that, in an atlas, is the use of a benchmark.

For Tennessee, the national health figures are the frame; the state's own distribution, tabled below, is the picture inside it. Employer-sponsored insurance is the largest single source, covering about half of Black Americans, through their own jobs or a family member's — a share below the White figure, reflecting the employment and occupational data of this reference: fewer Black workers in the large-firm, high-benefit sectors, more in service, temporary, and small-employer jobs where offers of coverage are thinner. Two sides frame the comparison here: the national midpoint, and the households of Tennessee.

Reading This Plate

How to read this plate: the state rows are the published census figures for Tennessee; the benchmark rows are the published national health record, labeled as benchmarks. In Tennessee, health measures are survey-based, so the plate carries the survey sources on its face and treats the national pattern as the frame inside which state health agencies publish their own state-level readings.

The Population Behind the Plate

The coverage figures take their local form in Tennessee, where the state rows below set the population behind the national pattern. Tennessee holds about 1.25 million Black residents — Black alone or in combination — nearly one in five of its people, with Black alone at about 1.13 million, or 16 percent. Locating Tennessee is the benchmark's purpose; ranking it is not.

In Tennessee, coverage is the gateway measure: it decides whether the rest of the health system is reachable at ordinary cost. The national Black coverage record, carried here as the benchmark, is the product of federal and state policy layered over the population this atlas counts — and in Tennessee that population numbers 1.25 million. In Tennessee, prevalence measures, from hypertension to diabetes, sit downstream of coverage, which is why this plate leads with the insurance record.
