Age and Households: The National Plate

Every rate in this atlas has a denominator, and this plate draws the denominator itself: the age structure of Black America and the households that structure forms. The Black population is younger than the nation it is part of. Its median age stands at about thirty-five years, against about thirty-nine for the total population; children under eighteen form roughly twenty-two percent of the Black population, some nine to ten million children; and adults sixty-five and over form about eleven to twelve percent, a share that has been rising steadily and will keep rising across the coming decades. The plate reads that structure geographically, because age, like everything else in this atlas, has a map.

The geography of age

The youngest Black populations are found in the fast-growing metros of the South and in Utah's small, young, and rapidly growing Black community, where median ages read in the low thirties or below; the oldest are found in the slow-growing or declining geographies — parts of the rural Black Belt, West Virginia, and the older industrial counties of the Midwest — where the young have left and the median age climbs into the forties. The pattern matters because it silently shapes every other plate: a geography's income median, its degree share, its health outcomes, and its homeownership rate all bend with its age structure, and the atlas's methodology notes flag the places where an apparent difference between two states is, in measurable part, a difference in who is young and who is old. The state plates carry each state's median age and broad age bands in their tables so the reader can make that adjustment with the numbers in hand.

The households they form

From that age structure, roughly fifteen to sixteen million Black households form. The American Community Survey's household tables describe their shapes: married-couple households, householders living with extended family at rates above the national pattern, single householders, and a substantial share of households with children present — about a third. Multigenerational households, in which three or more generations or grandparents and grandchildren share a roof, are a standing feature of the Black household map rather than an exception, and they concentrate where housing costs, caregiving, and tradition intersect — in the big metros and the deep South alike. The thematic household plates draw these arrangements state by state, because household shape is the mechanism that connects the population plates to the housing and income plates: it is households, not persons, that earn the median income and hold the tenure this atlas maps.

How to read a structure plate

Structure plates reward slow reading, because their figures explain more than they announce. A state's low median income may be a young population's income; a low homeownership rate may be a renter-heavy young metro's arithmetic; a health outcome may bend with an older county's age mix. The atlas does not adjust its headline figures for age except where the source publishes adjusted rates — the health plates do, the income plates do not — and it states the practice on every table, so the reader always knows whether a comparison is raw or adjusted. What this plate guarantees is that the raw material of the adjustment is always available one click away: the age bands and household counts for every geography in the atlas, drawn from the same censuses and surveys as everything else, and cited to them. Begin any deep reading of a place here, and the rest of its plates will argue with each other less and explain each other more.

Structure as the atlas's foundation

This plate is the foundation the other five hundred and ninety-nine stand on, and it is deliberately placed among the first a reader meets. The state plates repeat its core figures in miniature — median age, the child share, the older share, the household count — at the head of each state's table, so no state's six plates are ever read without its structure in view. The thematic plates then take the structure itself as their subject: the geography of Black childhood, county by county where the counts support it; the map of the older population and the multigenerational households that surround it; the household-type plates that show how Black America arranges its roofs. As the decades turn, this is also the plate that will change most consequentially: the rising older share will redraw the health and housing maps, and the child share will keep forecasting the education map a generation ahead. An atlas that draws structure honestly is an atlas that can be re-read as a forecast; that is the spirit in which these figures are set down, sourced to the census and the survey, and left to speak.
