Black Richmond: Concentration and Share

In Richmond, concentration is the metro question in its purest form: how much of a place is Black, and how that share compares with the state and the nation around it. In the Richmond metro, Black residents number about 105,000 and form 46% of the population on the alone-or-in-combination count — against 19% for the state as a whole on the alone count, and 13.5% for the United States.

The Share and What It Builds

A share is not a statistic in the abstract; at 46%, it is the composition of every school, congregation, electorate-sized gathering, and customer base in the metro. In Richmond, shares at this level sustain institutions — media, congregations, businesses, and political organizations — that smaller shares cannot, and they shape how a metro appears in every national table this atlas publishes. The Richmond figure also explains the metro's gravitational pull in the movement: concentration is itself an attraction, drawing new residents toward an established community with its institutions already built.

Reading This

The definitions differ by the multiracial population — a real and growing count, small against shares like Richmond's. Residents moving between states — domestic migration — are the stream that built and rebuilt Black geographies across a century and a half, Richmond's among them. Richmond's place in that ordering is its concentration signature.

The distribution reads at state scale in Richmond, where the table's rows give the published count and the share the map draws. The capital region's 330,000 Black residents distribute across the independent city's ring in the Virginia pattern. Henrico County, the suburban giant to the north and west, holds a Black population in the range of 100,000 — roughly three-tenths of the county — from the older East End communities of Sandston and Highland Springs to the new subdivisions of the far East End, one of the largest suburban Black populations of any county in the South.
