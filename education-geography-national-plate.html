Education Geography: The National

Education is the measure on which the Black map has changed fastest within living memory, and this draws its current national form. In recent American Community Survey readings, about three in ten Black adults aged twenty-five and over hold a bachelor's degree or higher — a share that has climbed steadily across every recent decade. The geography of that attainment is neither the geography of population share nor of population count: it concentrates where professional economies and great universities sit, thins across the rural South and Appalachia, and rearranges itself interestingly when read by metropolitan area rather than by state.

The attainment map

The District of Columbia leads the nation, with about four in ten Black adults holding at least a bachelor's degree — a professional city's figure. Massachusetts, Colorado, Maryland, Virginia, and New Jersey form the next tier, each in the mid-to-high thirties in recent readings. The pattern is partly institutional: these are states of dense higher-education and professional employment, and their Black populations are selected by the same labor markets that draw degree-holders of every background. At the map's low end, West Virginia, Mississippi, and Louisiana record attainment near one in five. Between the tiers sit the large states — Georgia and North Carolina near the national figure on the strength of their metros, Texas and Florida near it on scale, New York and Illinois pulled in two directions by their great cities and their upstate and downstate remainders.

The pattern inside the pattern

Two internal patterns recur on every education in this atlas. The first is the lead of Black women: across the nation, Black women aged twenty-five and over hold bachelor's degrees at a rate roughly six points above Black men, and they form a majority of Black enrollment and Black degree completions at every level — a gap the thematic draw state by state, where in several states it runs wider still. The second is the metro concentration of attainment: within nearly every state, the metropolitan Black population's degree share stands well above the nonmetropolitan share, in some states by more than ten points, which is why the Metro carry an attainment reading for each of the forty. The State section's education assemble both patterns with the enrollment and completion figures published by the National Center for Education Statistics, so attainment is read alongside the pipeline that produces it.

How to read an education

Attainment is a stock, not a flow: it counts everyone aged twenty-five and over, including generations educated when access was far narrower, so the national figure understates the attainment of younger cohorts considerably — among Black adults in their late twenties and early thirties, the degree share reads several points higher. The note the age frame on every table for exactly this reason. Figures are drawn from the American Community Survey and the National Center for Education Statistics as published, with the survey vintage named. What the cannot measure — school quality, field of study's earnings, the difference a degree makes in a particular labor market — belongs to the income' territory, and the atlas keeps the two in conversation rather than pretending either one answers alone.

Education inside the atlas

Attainment returns throughout the atlas in company with the measures it feeds. The state education carry, beside the degree shares, the enrollment picture at the state's historically Black colleges and universities where they exist — institutions that educate a share of Black graduates far beyond their share of enrollment nationally, and whose locations, from Atlanta and Nashville to Greensboro and Baton Rouge, are themselves features of the education map. The thematic cut attainment by field of degree and by cohort, and set the graduate and professional tier — roughly one Black adult in twenty-five holds an advanced degree in recent readings — on its own. Read with the income, the education geography explains a large part of the earnings ladder; read with the movement, it explains where the young and credentialed are going. The national narrower job is done if the reader carries away its three standing facts: attainment near three in ten and climbing, concentrated in the professional states and metros, and led, in every state in the union, by Black women.
