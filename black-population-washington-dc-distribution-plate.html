Black Washington: The District and the Metro

This metro plate opens the Atlasmont file on Black Washington: the population itself — its size, its share of the metro, and its distribution across the city and suburbs that make up the metropolitan whole.

The subject here is Black Washington: The District and the Metro, and the reading is the atlas's standard one: the published figures first, the interpretation after. The metropolitan area holds about 1.6 million Black residents — roughly one in four — while the District of Columbia itself, which was the first large American city to become majority Black, in the 1950s, now records a Black share of about 41 percent: still the highest of any major American city jurisdiction, but no longer a majority. The District's Black share peaked at about 71 percent in 1970, the product of a century and a half of Black Washington — a free Black community predating the Civil War, a Reconstruction-era influx, Great Migration arrivals, and the mid-twentieth-century departure of White residents to the suburbs. From the 1990s onward, the direction reversed: the city's population grew for the first time in decades on an influx of younger, affluent, disproportionately White newcomers, while Black Washingtonians moved to the suburbs in large numbers — many priced out of neighborhoods their families had inhabited for generations. Reading the Washington Numbers

East of the River

Inside the District, the census maps a divide along the Anacostia River. Wards 7 and 8, east of the river, remain overwhelmingly Black — above 90 percent in many tracts — and record the city's lowest incomes and its most difficult health indicators, while the wards west of Rock Creek Park have become majority White and affluent. The river line is among the sharpest internal demographic boundaries of any American city, and tract-level profiles in this section document it in full.

Washington's data punish careless reading in a specific way: the city and the region tell opposite stories. Quoting the District alone yields decline — falling share, displacement, a lost majority. Quoting the metro yields continuity and growth — 1.6 million Black residents, rising suburban affluence, the strongest Black professional base of any region its size. Both are true, and the truth of the profile is in holding them together: a population that did not leave Washington so much as redistribute across it, along lines of income and housing cost that the census maps precisely. The profiles that follow — Chicago, Houston, and the rest — apply the same discipline of separating the city figure from the regional one.
