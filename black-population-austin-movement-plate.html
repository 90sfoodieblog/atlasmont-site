Black Austin: Growth and Movement

In Austin, metros are where the national movement story is actually decided — the gains and losses of a few dozen large communities add up to the country's direction. This plate reads the movement record of Black Austin, a metro of about 85,000 Black residents, against the currents that have reshaped Black geography in the present era.

Growth and Movement

The migration currents read locally in Austin, where the state rows below set the population whose movement the national pattern frames. Austin holds about 85,000 Black residents within the city — Black alone or in combination, roughly 9 percent of its 960,000 people — and about 155,000 across the metropolitan area. National material earns its place on this plate only where it sharpens Austin's own record, stated plainly in the table.

For Austin, the national movement figures are the current; the state's own distribution, tabled below, is where the current lands. Austin's Black share fell from roughly 12 percent in 1990 to about 9 percent in combination counts today, even as the city's total population nearly doubled; the east-side tracts nearest downtown record Black shares collapsing from majorities to small minorities within two decades; and the Black population's center moved to the far northeast and the suburbs — Pflugerville (now roughly a fifth Black in combination counts, the metro's suburban Black center), Manor, Round Rock's east side, Del Valle, and the Colony Park and Dessau corridors inside the city. The comparison the plate invites is therefore two-sided: the national currents on one side, the state's arrivals and departures on the other.

The Currents Around the Metro

In Austin, the present era's currents run in several directions at once: toward the South and its suburbs, out of the highest-cost coastal metros, and into mid-sized metros whose Black populations are growing fastest in percentage terms. The Austin metro's record, in the published counts, is its own negotiation among those currents. In Austin, each decennial census and each survey year revises the picture at the margin; the direction of the lines is the story, and this plate is built to be re-read as the lines extend.

In Austin, that national movement pattern arrives as local gain or loss, and the table below fixes the population it describes. Black Austin was placed where it is by policy: the city's 1928 master plan designated the east side as the Negro district (conditioning city services on residence there), and East Austin — the streets around East 11th and 12th, Rosewood, and the districts toward the old airport — held the community's churches, its businesses (the Victory Grill's Chitlin' Circuit stage among them), and its institutions for the rest of the century. Held against the national ladder, Austin's own rows say where its attainment stands.

Movement into and out of the Austin metro is measured in the federal record as the difference between counts, and its streams — births and deaths inside the metro, arrivals from other American places, arrivals from abroad — are published separately in the estimates program.
