Black Denver: Growth and Movement

In Denver, metros are where the national movement story is actually decided — the gains and losses of a few dozen large communities add up to the country's direction. This plate reads the movement record of Black Denver, a metro of about 65,000 Black residents, against the currents that have reshaped Black geography in the present era.

Growth and Movement

The migration currents read locally in Denver, where the state rows below set the population whose movement the national pattern frames. For Denver, five Points, the neighborhood northeast of downtown named for the five-way intersection at its heart, was Black Denver's center from the early twentieth century — the district where Black families, excluded by covenant from most of the growing city, built their churches, businesses, and one of the West's great jazz scenes. That is what a benchmark is for in an atlas: not to rank Denver, but to locate it.

Park Hill, the grand early suburb east of City Park, integrated in the 1950s and 1960s — its fair-housing fights are a documented chapter of the era — and became the Black professional class's address, a status its North Park Hill census tracts retain. Montbello, the planned far-northeast district built from the 1960s, became the city's largest Black neighborhood of the late century, a homeowner suburb inside the city line. A benchmark in an atlas does not rank Denver; it locates it, and that is its whole use here.

The Currents Around the Metro

In Denver, the present era's currents run in several directions at once: toward the South and its suburbs, out of the highest-cost coastal metros, and into mid-sized metros whose Black populations are growing fastest in percentage terms. The Denver metro's record, in the published counts, is its own negotiation among those currents. In Denver, each decennial census and each survey year revises the picture at the margin; the direction of the lines is the story, and this plate is built to be re-read as the lines extend.
