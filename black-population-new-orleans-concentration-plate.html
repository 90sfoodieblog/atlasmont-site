Black New Orleans: Concentration and Share

In New Orleans, concentration is the metro question in its purest form: how much of a place is Black, and how that share compares with the state and the nation around it. In the New Orleans metro, Black residents number about 230,000 and form 59% of the population on the alone-or-in-combination count — against 33% for the state as a whole on the alone count, and 13.5% for the United States.

The Share and What It Builds

A share is not a statistic in the abstract; at 59%, it is the composition of every school, congregation, electorate-sized gathering, and customer base in the metro. In New Orleans, shares at this level sustain institutions — media, congregations, businesses, and political organizations — that smaller shares cannot, and they shape how a metro appears in every national table this atlas publishes. The New Orleans figure also explains the metro's gravitational pull in the movement: concentration is itself an attraction, drawing new residents toward an established community with its institutions already built.

New Orleans holds about 230,000 Black residents — Black alone or in combination, roughly three in five of the city's 384,000 people — and a metropolitan Black population above 400,000. In the census of 2000, New Orleans was two-thirds Black, a city of 484,000; Hurricane Katrina's flood in 2005 emptied it, and the population that returned over the following fifteen years was smaller and differently composed. Black New Orleans before Katrina was the product of three centuries: a colonial city where free people of color held property and formed militias under French and Spanish rule; the greatest slave market of the domestic trade; and then, after emancipation, a Black working-class city of extraordinary cultural generation — the birthplace of jazz in its Tremé and Seventh Ward neighborhoods, of the Mardi Gras Indian tradition, of second-line parades and social aid and pleasure clubs, institutions built by and for the Black working class.
