Movement and Change: Black Alaska

Populations move, and the census counts the result. This plate reads the movement record of Black Alaska — the growth, the arrivals, and the departures written into the published counts for the state, whose Black population stands at 50,000, alone or in combination, 3 percent of the state on the alone count.

Growth and Migration

The composition follows the Pacific pattern of Hawaii's file: a large military share with high turnover (Alaska's Black population moves in and out on orders, and the census's mobility data show it), over a rooted civilian layer — the highway generation's descendants, the pipeline-era arrivals of the 1970s (the trans-Alaska pipeline's construction drew Black workers in the boom and some stayed), the fishing and maritime workforce, and a growing African immigrant community in Anchorage. The multiracial share is high, as in Hawaii: Black-Native and Black-white combination counts are substantial in the urban data, and the 'in combination' convention is again the honest headline.

The indicators carry the federal-economy cushion of the other garrison states: employment and incomes anchored to military and public scales, education through the University of Alaska system, housing costs in Anchorage's severe market borne partly through service allowances. The estimates show the Black population stable to slowly growing with force levels, its rooted share rising slowly as each military generation leaves a retiree layer behind. Alaska Native corporations and the state's distinctive economic institutions touch the Black population mainly as employers; the two communities' data otherwise run on separate tracks in the state's tables, meeting in the mixed neighborhoods of the east side.

The National Movement Around the State

In Alaska, black movement in the present era runs in several directions at once: out of the largest older metros, toward the South and its suburbs, and into mid-sized cities whose Black populations are growing fastest in percentage terms. Alaska, in the West, sits inside that wider current. The decennial counts and the survey estimates between them are the instruments that register the change; each new release revises the picture at the margin, and the direction of the lines — which places gain, which hold, which thin — is the story this plate is built to tell for Alaska.

In Alaska, movement is measured three ways in the federal record, and the plate distinguishes them. In Alaska, natural increase adds births and subtracts deaths inside the state. What separates the two definitions is the multiracial population — real, growing, and small against shares of the size recorded in Alaska. In Alaska, international migration adds residents born abroad, a stream that has reshaped the Black populations of the largest gateway metros and, increasingly, of states well beyond them. For Alaska, the published components separate the streams, and the balance among them is the state's movement signature.

For Alaska, the national movement figures are the current; the state's own distribution, tabled below, is where the current lands. Alaska holds about 50,000 Black residents — Black alone or in combination — roughly one in fifteen of its people, with Black alone at about 24,000, or 3.3 percent. Alaska's own rows, tabled below, are where this general material becomes a specific place.

The migration currents read locally in Alaska, where the state rows below set the population whose movement the national pattern frames. When the Army built the Alaska Highway in 1942 — 1,500 miles of road through the Yukon and British Columbia in eight months — roughly a third of the soldiers who built it were Black, serving in segregated engineer regiments (the 93rd, 95th, and 97th Engineers and their sister units) assigned disproportionately to the northern, coldest, least-supplied sections. The general record is background; the local one — Alaska's, in the table — is the subject.

Reading This Plate

In Alaska, read the table from the top: the share first, because the share is what the map shades; then the count, because the count is what the share is made of. Shares and counts answer different questions. The share says how present Black life is in the everyday population of Alaska; the count says how much of Black America lives there. Both are published figures, rounded as the source agency publishes them, and both are the ground every other Alaska plate in this atlas stands on.

What follows is the plate the atlas files as Movement and Change: Black Alaska.
