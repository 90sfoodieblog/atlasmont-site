The Oregon Income Plate

In Oregon, income is where the atlas's population counts meet household economics. This plate places Black Oregon in the national income geography: the benchmarks below are the published national figures for Black households, and Oregon's own distribution figures — 195,000 Black residents, 2 percent of the state — give the scale of the community those national patterns describe within the state's borders.

The National Pattern

The income figures take their local meaning in Oregon, where the table's state rows set the population this benchmark describes. Median household income is the most widely used single measure of a population's economic position, and for Black households in the United States the Census Bureau's annual income report places the figure at $56,490. Not to rank Oregon but to locate it — that, in an atlas, is the use of a benchmark.

For Oregon, the national benchmark is a yardstick, not a description; the description is the state's own distribution, tabled below. Household income counts the combined money income of everyone aged 15 and over living in the home — wages, salaries, self-employment income, Social Security, pensions, interest, and cash benefits — before taxes. The comparison the plate invites is therefore two-sided: the national midpoint on one side, Oregon's households on the other.

The national median household income in the same report is $77,719. Against that benchmark, the Black median of $56,490 is about 73 cents on the dollar. The Population Behind the Plate

In Oregon, that national income pattern is read against the state's own households, whose published share and count stand in the table below. Oregon holds about 195,000 Black residents — Black alone or in combination — roughly one in twenty-two of its people, with Black alone at about 92,000, or 2.2 percent. The exclusion clause of 1857 — a dead letter after the Fourteenth Amendment, unrepealed in text until 1926 — capped the Black population for a century; the modern community, centered in Portland, is the product of one wartime boom and everything that has happened since its erasure. The table is where the general pattern becomes Oregon's own figures.

The income figures take their local meaning in Oregon, where the table's state rows set the population this benchmark describes. The clause's effect is legible in the census series themselves: Oregon's Black population in 1900 numbered barely a thousand, in a state of over 400,000 — the smallest Black share of any state outside the northern Rockies, maintained by the provisional government's earlier exclusion laws (1844 and 1849), by the clause, and, after its legal death, by the sundown practices and the organized white-supremacist presence of the 1920s, in which Oregon's chapter was among the nation's largest per capita. The tiny community that persisted — Portland's railroad porters and hotel workers along the old Albina waterfront, a congregation tradition dating to the 1860s — lived its whole early history under the founding document's shadow. Only as it sharpens the local record does the general one matter here — and the local record is Oregon's, in the table.

Residents moving between states — domestic migration — are the stream that built and rebuilt Black geographies across a century and a half, Oregon's among them. Read against the population counted on the distribution plate, the national median describes the economic center of gravity of the community that Oregon's 195,000 Black residents belong to. Holding the structure still while the numbers move is the gazetteer's work — the only way an atlas stays honest across editions, for Oregon as for every entry.
