Health Geography: The National

This turns the atlas to health, and it does so in the atlas's own terms: outcomes, mapped, from the published federal health record, without commentary on causes the data cannot carry. The headline measure is life expectancy at birth, which recent readings from the National Center for Health Statistics place at about seventy-two to seventy-three years for Black Americans, against about seventy-eight for the total population. Around that headline sit the major outcome measures the federal surveys track — blood pressure, diabetes, infant and maternal outcomes, insurance coverage — each with its own geography, drawn here at national scale and then state by state in the State section.

The regional pattern

Black life expectancy is not one number but a regional range. Readings run highest in the Northeast corridor and in parts of the West, and lowest across portions of the deep South and the industrial Midwest, where in some states the Black figure has read in the high sixties in recent releases. The state spread — on the order of six to eight years between the highest and lowest readings — is among the widest internal ranges in this atlas. Infant mortality follows a related geography, with the highest Black rates recorded in the Midwest and South and the lowest in the West and Northeast. The state health carry each state's own readings with the survey and vital-statistics vintages named, because health statistics are revised and restated more often than any other series this atlas uses.

The measured conditions

Among the measured conditions, hypertension is the most prevalent: federal examination surveys read elevated blood pressure in roughly fifty-five to fifty-eight percent of Black adults, the highest of any major population group the surveys publish. Diabetes prevalence reads in the low-to-mid teens percent, and obesity measures in the examination surveys read higher still. Insurance coverage — the measure nearest to a policy lever that this atlas records — expanded across the last decade and a half, and its state geography now tracks closely which states operate expanded Medicaid programs, a correspondence the state health make visible without editorializing it. Each condition's in the Thematic section gathers the state readings into one view, so a reader can compare the blood-pressure map against the life-expectancy map and see where they rhyme and where they do not.

How to read a health

Health statistics require the atlas's strictest reading habits. Rates are age-adjusted where the source publishes adjusted figures, because populations of different age structures cannot be compared raw; the state the adjustment on every table. Small populations produce unstable rates, and the state for the smallest Black populations carry the source's own reliability flags rather than false precision. Above all, the record outcomes, not explanations: they show where life runs shorter and where measured conditions concentrate, drawn from the vital statistics and examination surveys of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and they leave causal argument to the literature the citations point toward. An atlas that maps outcomes honestly, at full geographic grain, has done the work its readers — researchers, journalists, policymakers — most need from it.

Health inside the atlas

Health is the section of the atlas where the state grain matters most, and the State are built accordingly: each state's health pairs its life-expectancy reading with its infant mortality rate, its measured-condition prevalences, and its insurance coverage, all from the federal sources named on the. The thematic health gather the single measures across all fifty-one geographies, so that the diabetes map can be laid beside the hypertension map and both beside the map of coverage. The Black Belt & Regions section carries the county-level readings where the vital statistics support them, because the rural South's outcomes are the sharpest on the national map and a state average hides them. Throughout, the atlas holds its line: outcomes only, fully sourced, age-adjusted where the source adjusts, with reliability flags where populations are small. The reader who needs causes will find, in each citation, the doorway to the federal literature that argues them; the atlas's contribution is the geography itself, drawn carefully enough to argue from.
