Black Population in Greenwood, Mississippi

Greenwood is a city of about 14,500 people, the seat of Leflore County where the Mississippi Delta's flat cotton land meets the hills along the Yazoo River — and about 74 percent of its residents are Black. Greenwood was for decades one of the world's great cotton markets, a trading city whose wealth was built by Black field labor across Leflore and the surrounding counties, and its Black population today descends directly from that labor force. It is also a city of unusual industrial distinction: Viking Range, the premium appliance maker founded in Greenwood in the 1980s, built its headquarters and factories in the city's downtown. This article profiles Greenwood's Black population in the census record.

Size and Share

The 2020 census counted roughly 10,700 Black residents in Greenwood, about 74 percent of the city, within a Leflore County that is about 72 percent Black — one of the highest county shares in Mississippi. The city's population peaked in the cotton era at more than 20,000 and has declined steadily as plantation labor disappeared and the Delta's young people left for Jackson, Memphis, and the metropolitan South. The Black share, meanwhile, has remained high and stable: Greenwood has been a majority-Black city for generations, its Black neighborhoods laid out in the cotton era along the river and the rail lines, its Black institutions — churches, schools, the historic Black business stretches — continuous through the whole of the twentieth century. The surrounding Delta counties, from Humphreys to Tallahatchie, show the same pattern, and Greenwood serves as one of their commercial capitals.

The Cotton Capital and Its People

Greenwood's nickname in its boom years was the cotton capital of the world: the bales of the eastern Delta came to its compresses and warehouses, and its Cotton Row of brokers and buyers fronted Grand Boulevard, often described as one of the most beautiful residential streets in the South. Every bale in that trade passed through Black hands — the hands of the enslaved people who built the levees and cleared the Delta bottoms, and of the sharecroppers and wage laborers who succeeded them. The city's Black history runs through the whole Delta story: the Great Migration drew thousands from Leflore County to Chicago; the movement era brought national attention to the county; and Greenwood's own musical inheritance — it is a hometown of blues and gospel traditions carried worldwide — runs alongside Clarksdale's. The founding of Viking Range in the 1980s, and its later development of the Cotton Row district into hotels and restaurants around the factory, gave the old cotton city a second economic identity unusual in the Delta — one built, fittingly, on making the stoves the region's kitchens made famous.

Income, Industry, and the Present Economy

Greenwood's median household income is about $29,500, roughly 30 percent of residents live in poverty, and about 16 percent of adults hold a bachelor's degree or higher. Employment rests on a base narrower than the city's history would suggest: the Viking Range factories and the hospitality development around them, the Greenwood Leflore Hospital, the public schools and county government, Milwaukee Tool's distribution and manufacturing presence in the area, and the remaining agricultural economy of Leflore County's large farms. Mississippi Valley State University, the historically Black university founded in 1950, stands a few miles east at Itta Bena and forms part of the same Leflore County community — its students, faculty, and events are woven into Greenwood's Black civic life. The census picture is of a Delta city whose Black population carries both the region's deep history and its hard economics: a 74 percent majority sustaining a full institutional life on the eastern Delta's edge. The Leflore County figures extend the pattern in every direction. The county's Black population, at roughly 72 percent, is spread between Greenwood, the small towns along the highways, and a countryside still organized by the great farms — and its institutions reflect that spread: county-wide congregations, a consolidated school district, and family landholdings that in some cases run back to the first generations after emancipation. Greenwood's role in that county is the role this article has described throughout: the market town, the hospital town, and the county seat of one of the Blackest counties in Mississippi, whose people built the Delta's wealth and whose descendants, at 74 percent of the city, remain its majority and its center of gravity.
