The Black Population of North Dakota

About 42,000 Black North Dakota residents — one in nineteen — in the fastest-transforming small population in the northern plains. The census profile.

Distribution Across the State

In North Dakota, black North Dakota's early record is the plains pattern at its thinnest: Black soldiers at Fort Abraham Lincoln and Fort Totten, porters and barbers in the railroad towns (Fargo, Grand Forks, Bismarck, Minot), a small Fargo congregation tradition, and individual homesteaders in the settlement era — a population the censuses counted in the hundreds through the territory's first decades and in the low thousands across the twentieth century. The community's historic institutions — Fargo's and Grand Forks' Black churches, the state's small NAACP tradition — carried a presence far steadier than its size, and the population's stability (rather than disappearance, as in many plains counties) owed to the railroad division points and the state university towns.

The second and third layers are extractive and military. The Bakken shale boom drew Black workers to the Williston Basin in the 2000s and 2010s — the oil counties' Black counts multiplied from near zero in the boom censuses, in the Wyoming energy pattern at greater amplitude — and the bust cycles thinned them in turn. The Air Force anchors the permanent layer: Grand Forks AFB and Minot AFB sustain Black service communities in their cities (Grand Forks and Minot record sustained shares in the mid-single digits), and the missile fields' support towns add a dispersed increment. Bismarck, the capital, holds a small government-shaped community; the reservation counties and the farm belt record the plains minimum.

The State on the National Plate

On the national plate, North Dakota sits in the lightest band on the plate, under 6 percent. Its Black alone share of 4 percent — 37 of the fifty-one geographies mapped — stands against a national figure of 13.5 percent, and its Black population, 42,000 alone or in combination, is one of the building blocks of the national total of 52.8 million. An atlas reads North Dakota two ways at once: by the size of its community, and by that community's weight inside the whole population. North Dakota is legible on both readings, and this section of the atlas holds the two side by side.

Where the general meets the particular is the table of North Dakota; read the two together. In North Dakota's band — the lightest band on the plate, under 6 percent — the shaded peers include Alaska, Arizona, Colorado and Hawaii. In North Dakota, banding is a cartographer's compromise: it trades precision for pattern, letting the eye take in the geography of a whole country at once. The table restores the precision the shading rounds away, carrying the published share and count for North Dakota exactly as the source agency releases them.

In North Dakota, every figure on this plate descends from the federal count of the whole population — the decennial census and the American Community Survey that updates it between census years. Those instruments count everyone, which is what makes a share meaningful: 4 percent is not a sample of Black North Dakota, it is Black North Dakota measured against all of North Dakota. In North Dakota, the atlas's other plates return to this population again and again, because income, schooling, health, and ownership are all attributes of the people counted here, in this place, first.

North Dakota's profile is the plains' version of Maine's: a state whose Black population was, within living memory, a rounding error, and is now a visible, growing, Fargo-centered community of 42,000. The census's long series is the story — read the 1990 and 2020 tables side by side and the whole modern Black geography of the northern plains is in the two columns.

Reading This Plate

In North Dakota, read the table from the top: the share first, because the share is what the map shades; then the count, because the count is what the share is made of. Shares and counts answer different questions. The share says how present Black life is in the everyday population of North Dakota; the count says how much of Black America lives there. Both are published figures, rounded as the source agency publishes them, and both are the ground every other North Dakota plate in this atlas stands on.

A note on the two counts this atlas uses throughout. In North Dakota, the Black alone count holds residents who marked Black and no other race; the alone-or-in-combination count adds residents who marked Black alongside another race. In North Dakota, the second is the larger and the more inclusive, and the gap between them is itself a fact about the place — widest where multiracial identification is most common, narrowest where it is rare. For North Dakota, the alone share of 4 percent drives the shading, while the combination count of 42,000 carries the community's full enumerated size.
