Filed in this series as Black Population in Georgia: One in Three Georgians, the plate below states the published record for its subject and reads it closely. Its table records Black residents of Georgia, alone or in combination: 3.6 Million; roughly one in three Georgians is Black — among the highest shares of any large state: 1 in 3. In Georgia, that is the whole of the plate's claim, and it is enough for a reference.

In Georgia, read as a region, the pattern crosses the state borders the other plates observe, and the table below fixes its measures. The decennial census counted about 3.6 million Black Georgians — Black alone or in combination — roughly one in three residents of the state, with Black alone at about 3.3 million, or 31 percent. The comparison the plate invites is therefore two-sided: the national ladder on one side, Georgia's attainment on the other.

Historical Roots

In Georgia, the regional series holds its geography whole; the state plates elsewhere in the atlas divide the same ground by state lines. Georgia was the last of the original colonies to legalize slavery and then built one of the largest enslaved populations in the South; by the Civil War, nearly half of Georgians were enslaved. That is what a benchmark is for in an atlas: not to rank Georgia, but to locate it.

Reading the Georgia Numbers

Georgia rewards the two-measure discipline this reference applies everywhere: share and scale. By share, Georgia at roughly one in three is exceeded among states only by Mississippi and Louisiana and by the District of Columbia as a city jurisdiction; by scale, its 3.6 million rank near the very top. Averages require the most care of all here, because the state's Black average blends a booming, credentialed metropolitan majority with a rural Black Belt whose indicators resemble the Deep South's hardest counties. The state profiles in this section are built to keep those layers separate, and Georgia is the reason why.

Georgia's profile is the template case for the state series in this section: a state where the Black population is at once an old population, rooted in counties its families have inhabited for two centuries, and a new one, arriving by the tens of thousands to a metropolitan region that now defines Black America's center of gravity as much as any place in the country.
