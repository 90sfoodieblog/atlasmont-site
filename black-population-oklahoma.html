The Two Metros

In Oklahoma, read as a region, the pattern crosses the state borders the other observe, and the table below fixes its measures. Oklahoma City holds the larger share: the capital city is roughly 15 to 16 percent Black (some 105,000 residents), centered historically on the Deep Deuce and the northeast side, with a metro Black population in the range of 155,000 spread through Midwest City (the Tinker Air Force Base suburb, roughly a fifth Black), Del City, Spencer, and the northeast suburbs. Tulsa, the second metro, holds about 110,000 Black residents in its metro — the city itself roughly a sixth Black — concentrated in North Tulsa, the districts beyond the railroad-and-highway line that has divided the city's data for a century. Greenwood and the All-Black Towns

In Oklahoma, the regional series holds its geography whole; the state elsewhere in the atlas divide the same ground by state lines. Greenwood, Tulsa's Black district, was by 1921 the most prosperous Black business community in America — its hotels, theaters, professional offices, and hundreds of businesses earning it the 'Black Wall Street' name — until the 1921 Tulsa Race Massacre destroyed some 35 blocks of it, a destruction the state's own commission documented in full a century later; the district's modern form, rebuilt by its survivors and now anchored by the Greenwood Cultural Center and a history center at its edge, is the state's central heritage site. That is what a benchmark is for in an atlas: not to rank Oklahoma, but to locate it.

Langston and the Freedmen

For Oklahoma, this regional reads the same federal material at the scale of the region itself, and the table below carries its published measures. Langston University, founded in 1897 in the all-Black town of the same name, is the state's HBCU and the West's historically Black land-grant — enrollment in the range of 2,000 — anchoring the town that shares its name and the state's Black professional formation. The second distinctive thread is tribal: the Freedmen — the formerly enslaved people held by the Five Tribes and their descendants, citizens of the tribal nations under the 1866 treaties in provisions the tribal courts and federal rulings of the current era have been re-litigating and, in the Cherokee and other nations' recent decisions, substantially affirming — form a Black Oklahoma population inside the tribal data as well as the state's, concentrated in the eastern counties (Muskogee, Okmulgee, Tahlequah's region) where the tribal nations are headquartered. Here, on the for Oklahoma, held against the national counts, the state's own rows say how its business population stands.

Indicators and Change

Black Oklahoma's indicators run at the plains-South pattern: median income below the national Black figure, homeownership comparatively strong in the small-town and suburban data, attainment lifted by Langston and the metros' universities, and health outcomes at the difficult end of the public health tables (the state's overall health rankings pull every group's figures). The estimates show the metros gaining — Oklahoma City's suburbs fastest — the small towns continuing their long decline, and Tulsa's north side roughly stable. The energy economy's cycles register in the state data as they do in Texas's, moderated by the military and government anchors of the three metro economies.

Reading the Oklahoma Numbers

Oklahoma's profile is the West's deepest Black historical geography in a mid-sized modern population: 390,000 people whose map includes a world-famous district, thirteen surviving all-Black towns, an HBCU town, and a tribal-citizen population without parallel. The counts are modest; the density of significance is the highest in this section.
