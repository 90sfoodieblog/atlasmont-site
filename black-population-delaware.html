Black Population in Delaware: A Fifth of the First State

Filed in this series as Black Population in Delaware: A Fifth of the First State, the plate below states the published record for its subject and reads it closely. Its table records Black residents of Delaware, alone or in combination: 245,000; Delaware State University — the state's HBCU — founded as a land-grant institution: 1891. In Delaware, the figures are the argument; the text only keeps them in order.

In Delaware, read as a region, the pattern crosses the state borders the other plates observe, and the table below fixes its measures. Delaware holds about 245,000 Black residents — Black alone or in combination — nearly a quarter of the First State's people, with Black alone at about 218,000, or 22 percent. Delaware's own rows, tabled below, are where this general material becomes a specific place.

Wilmington: The Majority-Black Center

For Delaware, read as a region, the pattern crosses the state borders the other plates observe, and the table below fixes its measures. Wilmington, the state's largest city at about 71,000, is majority Black — roughly 56 to 58 percent in the modern counts — a corporate and banking city whose residential data invert its office towers' prosperity. The comparison the plate invites is therefore two-sided: the national midpoint on one side, Delaware's households on the other.

Dover and Downstate

Here, on the plate for Delaware, read as a region, the pattern crosses the state borders the other plates observe, and the table below fixes its measures. Dover, the capital at about 40,000, is roughly two-fifths Black, its community built around state government, the Air Force base, and Delaware State University. In Delaware, sussex County, the shore-and-poultry south, holds the third layer: a Black population in the mid-teens percent share, rooted in the old farm towns (Milford, Seaford, Georgetown, Laurel) and now joined to the poultry-processing economy whose workforce layers Black, Haitian, and Latino communities through the county's towns — one of the more distinctive composition files in the mid-Atlantic data. In Delaware, the comparison the plate invites is therefore two-sided: the national currents on one side, the state's arrivals and departures on the other.

Delaware State and the HBCU Anchor

In Delaware, the regional series holds its geography whole; the state plates elsewhere in the atlas divide the same ground by state lines. Delaware State University, founded in 1891 as the State College for Colored Students under the second Morrill Act, is the state's land-grant HBCU — enrollment in the range of 6,000 in recent counts, the state's second university, and the institutional center of Black Delaware: its alumni anchor the state's Black professional and public class, its Dover campus is the capital's second institution, and its recent research-university growth is tracked in the education section's HBCU data. That is what a benchmark is for in an atlas: not to rank Delaware, but to locate it.

Composition and Indicators

Black Delaware's indicators run mid-South-Atlantic: median income below the state's high corporate-economy average but above the national Black figure, homeownership comparatively strong in the suburban counties, attainment rising through Delaware State and the University of Delaware. The composition is predominantly native-born and old-rooted, with growing Haitian communities downstate and African communities in New Castle County. The estimates show steady growth led by Middletown and the southern New Castle suburbs — the Philadelphia metro's expansion reaching into the state — with Wilmington's total stable and its share of the state's Black population slowly declining as the suburbs gain.

Reading the Delaware Numbers

A Border State's Deep Roots

Delaware's Black community is among the oldest in the mid-Atlantic in continuous record: a border state that held slavery to the eve of emancipation while sheltering one of the East's most active Underground Railroad corridors — Wilmington's stations and the harbor pilots' networks are the documented core of the state's freedom history — and free Black communities in its towns that predate the republic. That depth shows in the modern data as rootedness: multigenerational residence, church and family networks of long standing in every county, and a political presence in the state's small legislature that reflects seniority as much as numbers.

Delaware reads as a city-state profile: a quarter of a small state, one dominant city, one HBCU, three counties. Its Black population's weight in its state's life — political, institutional, cultural — exceeds what 245,000 people usually carry, because the state around them is small enough for them to constitute it.
