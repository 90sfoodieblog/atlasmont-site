New Hampshire: Counties and Places

In New Hampshire, every atlas needs a page where a state stops being a shaded tile and becomes a list of places. This is that page for New Hampshire: its counties and communities in their order, the metros the atlas files separately, and the pattern they make together — a Black population of 35,000, alone or in combination, 2% of the state on the alone count, ranked 43 of fifty-one geographies by that share.

The State's Black Places

The atlas's place files record no large metro file inside New Hampshire's borders, and that absence is itself a distribution fact: this is a state whose Black population is spread across small cities, county seats, and countryside rather than gathered into one dominant metro. Its are read accordingly — the county pattern first, the urban pattern second — which makes New Hampshire a standing counterweight to the metro-first reading of Black America.

In New Hampshire, a state without a dominant Black metro is not a state without Black places. In New Hampshire, its communities are held in county seats and small cities whose names rarely reach national tables, and the county pattern — many small concentrations rather than one large one — produces a different civic texture: more, smaller institutions, spread across more ground. The New Hampshire are written to that texture deliberately, because a metro-shaped template laid over this state would misdescribe it.

Counties and the Pattern Between Places

Between the named metros lies the county pattern, and in New Hampshire it carries the older geography: county seats where Black settlement predates the metros, corridors along the historic routes of movement, and — in the state's band of the national map, shaded at 2% — the rural counties whose shares run higher than the state's average even as their counts run small. In New Hampshire, the tile map shades the state as one tile; this exists to say what the tile contains.

Read against the region, New Hampshire's place pattern is a Northeast pattern with its own inflection. The Northeast's Black geography is the subject of the regional in this atlas, and New Hampshire contributes 35,000 Black residents to it. In New Hampshire, the state's largest places anchor its contribution; its counties distribute it. In New Hampshire, both halves are on this page because both halves are the state.

How Places Are Counted Here

In New Hampshire, the place figures in this atlas come in two kinds, and the keeps them labeled. In New Hampshire, metro figures describe metropolitan statistical areas — counties grouped by commuting, as the federal system defines them — while the state figures describe the state whole. A metro can cross a state line; where one of New Hampshire's profiled metros does, its full count stands in the metro file and its New Hampshire portion stands in the state's story, and the tables say which is which. In New Hampshire, precision about boundaries is not pedantry in an atlas; it is the difference between a map and a picture of a map.

Using This

The Counties and Places is the index to everything else the atlas holds for New Hampshire: the six state for the whole population, the metro for each profiled place, and the gazetteer pages that open each place's file. In New Hampshire, a reader who begins here can reach any of them with the state's structure already in mind — which places are large, which are old, which are growing — and a reader who ends here has the whole state in one view. That double duty, index and summary at once, is what this seventh New Hampshire is for.

In New Hampshire, one further note belongs on the record of this: place data ages at different speeds. The definitions differ by the multiracial population — a real and growing count, small against shares like New Hampshire's. The figures here are the published ones as of the build of this atlas, and the structure they reveal — the ranking of New Hampshire's places, the weight of its metros against its counties — is built to survive the next revision of every number on the page.

The state's rank by share — 43 of fifty-one — is the figure the maps are drawn from, and this is where that rank becomes a population: 35,000 people, in the places listed above, in the proportions the county pattern gives them. In New Hampshire, rankings invite a league-table reading; the atlas resists it. A rank is an address, not a verdict, and New Hampshire's address in Black America is fixed by the same published counting that fixes every other state's.
