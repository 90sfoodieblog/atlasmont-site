The Black Belt

For Alabama, the regional series holds its geography whole; the state plates elsewhere in the atlas divide the same ground by state lines. The Black Belt arcs across Alabama's midsection from the Georgia line to Mississippi: a band of chalk-soil prairie counties — Wilcox, Lowndes, Dallas (whose seat is Selma), Greene, Sumter, Marengo, Hale, Perry, and their neighbors — where enslaved majorities worked the cotton economy and where Black majorities, in several counties exceeding 70 percent, persist today. Not to rank Alabama but to locate it — that, in an atlas, is the use of a benchmark.

Birmingham, Montgomery, Mobile

Birmingham, the state's largest metro, holds its largest Black population: the city itself is more than two-thirds Black — roughly 68 percent — and the metro's Black residents number in the several hundred thousands, suburbanized increasingly into the surrounding counties. The city's Black community was built by the steel and coal economy, and its institutions — Miles College, the Sixteenth Street Baptist Church, a large medical sector around the University of Alabama at Birmingham — anchor the north of the state. Montgomery, the capital, is roughly three-fifths Black and home to Alabama State University; its Black population carries the dual history of the Confederacy's first capital and the modern movement's first great victory, a juxtaposition the census maps in a city of 200,000. Mobile, the Gulf port, is just over half Black within the city, with a Black history reaching to the colonial Gulf Coast and the last slave ship's arrival in its bay.

Composition and Change

Read for Alabama, the regional series holds its geography whole; the state plates elsewhere in the atlas divide the same ground by state lines. For Alabama, the estimates describe a Black Alabama that is stable in share and slow-growing in count: natural increase offset by modest net out-migration to Georgia and Texas, an age structure older than the growth states', and a foreign-born share among the nation's smallest — a deep-rooted, native-born population in the same pattern as Mississippi's. In an atlas a benchmark answers one question — where does Alabama stand — and leaves ranking aside.

Reading the Alabama Numbers

In Alabama, alabama's state average, like Georgia's, blends regimes: a rural Black Belt at the hard end of the national distributions, three mid-sized metros in the middle, and one booming technology region. The share figure — more than one in four — and the HBCU density together define the profile: a state where Black life is demographically central and institutionally deep, working against economic indicators that the county tables beneath this profile document without softening.
