Black Oklahoma City: Concentration and Share

In Oklahoma City, concentration is the metro question in its purest form: how much of a place is Black, and how that share compares with the state and the nation around it. In the Oklahoma City metro, Black residents number about 110,000 and form 16% of the population on the alone-or-in-combination count — against 7% for the state as a whole on the alone count, and 13.5% for the United States.

The Share and What It Builds

A share is not a statistic in the abstract; at 16%, it is the composition of every school, congregation, electorate-sized gathering, and customer base in the metro. In Oklahoma City, shares at this level sustain institutions — media, congregations, businesses, and political organizations — that smaller shares cannot, and they shape how a metro appears in every national table this atlas publishes. The Oklahoma City figure also explains the metro's gravitational pull in the movement plates: concentration is itself an attraction, drawing new residents toward an established community with its institutions already built.

In Oklahoma, that pattern is the plate's whole subject: the table below gives the published share and count, and the map shades the share. Oklahoma City holds about 110,000 Black residents within the city — Black alone or in combination, roughly a sixth of its 681,000 people — and about 200,000 across the metropolitan area. Read back against the table, the general pattern resolves into Oklahoma City's particular numbers.

Reading This Plate

The definitions differ by the multiracial population — a real and growing count, small against shares like Oklahoma City's. Residents moving between states — domestic migration — are the stream that built and rebuilt Black geographies across a century and a half, Oklahoma City's among them. Oklahoma City's place in that ordering is its concentration signature.

For Oklahoma, the pattern is not background but subject; the state's own rows, tabled below, carry it. The employment structure is a frontier-capital blend: Tinker Air Force Base (the state's largest single-site employer, an air logistics center whose civilian workforce anchors the eastern suburbs' Black middle class), state government, the health systems, the energy sector's headquarters cycle (the city's booms and busts written into the employment series), and the logistics economy of the I-35/I-40 crossroads. Langston University — Oklahoma's only HBCU, founded 1897 in one of the all-Black towns, enrolling in the range of two thousand with an Oklahoma City campus in the northeast district — and the University of Central Oklahoma supply the higher-education layer, with Langston's existence tying the metro to the Black-town geography (Boley, the most famous of them, holds its rodeo and its data an hour east) that makes Oklahoma's Black history unlike any other state's. The comparison the plate invites is therefore two-sided: the national midpoint on one side, Oklahoma City's households on the other.

For Oklahoma, the pattern is not background but subject; the state's own rows, tabled below, carry it. The all-Black towns deserve the profile's own paragraph because they are Oklahoma's unique contribution to the national data: more than fifty incorporated Black towns and settlements founded between the 1860s and 1920s (Boley, Langston, Taft, Vernon among the thirteen surviving), promoted by Black townsite companies advertising in Southern Black newspapers — a frontier self-determination movement with no parallel elsewhere, whose remnants appear in the census as tiny majority-Black municipalities across the state's eastern half. In Oklahoma City, the comparison the plate invites is therefore two-sided: the national currents on one side, the state's arrivals and departures on the other.
