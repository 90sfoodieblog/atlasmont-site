Black Population in Tuskegee, Alabama

Tuskegee is a city of about 9,400 people, the seat of Macon County in east-central Alabama, and roughly 84 percent of its residents are Black. No small city in America carries a larger place in Black institutional history: Tuskegee University, founded in 1881 as a normal school for Black teachers and built into an international institution under Booker T. Washington, stands at the city's center, and the surrounding county is one of the most heavily Black in the United States. The census profile of Tuskegee is therefore the profile of a Black-majority Black Belt county seat whose life revolves around a historically Black university. This article sets out the city's Black population in the data: its size, the university that anchors it, and its economic indicators.

Size and Share

The 2020 census counted about 7,900 Black residents in Tuskegee, a share of roughly 84 percent, and the pattern extends across Macon County, which at about 78 percent Black is among the counties with the highest Black shares in Alabama. The city's population has been declining slowly for decades — Macon County's farm economy contracted through the twentieth century and younger residents leave for Auburn, Montgomery, and Atlanta — yet the Black share has remained at its high level throughout, because the community that stayed is the community that was always there. Tuskegee's age structure shows the university's fingerprint: a population bulge in the college years from Tuskegee University's roughly 3,000 students, layered over an older, deeply rooted residential population of families whose presence in Macon County predates the university itself.

The University City

Tuskegee's Black population is inseparable from the institution Booker T. Washington built. Founded in 1881 in a one-room shanty, the school became Tuskegee Institute and then Tuskegee University — home to George Washington Carver's agricultural research, to the Tuskegee Airmen's training fields in the Second World War, and to one of the proudest alumni networks in Black America. The university is the city's largest employer and its economic engine: its payroll, its students, and its visitors sustain a small city's economy, and its historic campus — a National Historic Site — draws heritage tourism to Macon County. The city also made constitutional history beyond the campus: the landmark Supreme Court case Gomillion v. Lightfoot (1960) arose in Tuskegee, striking down a redrawing of the city's boundaries that had been designed to fence Black residents out of the municipal electorate — an early judicial recognition that the city's Black majority was a political fact to be reckoned with. Few places of 9,400 people appear in the nation's civic record as often as this one.

Income, Education, and the County Around It

Tuskegee's median household income is about $30,500 and roughly 28 percent of residents live in poverty — figures that reflect Macon County's thin rural economy and that sit, strikingly, alongside an adult bachelor's-degree share of about 21 percent, elevated for the Black Belt by the university's faculty, staff, and graduates who settle in the city. That combination — high cultural and educational capital against low incomes — is Tuskegee's signature in the data, and it differs from the profile of Black Belt seats without a university. Employment beyond the university runs through the county school system, the veterans' medical center that has long stood in Tuskegee, municipal and county government, and commuting east to Auburn and Opelika, whose manufacturing and university economy is a half-hour away. The census record, in sum, describes a small, heavily Black city whose national importance is wildly disproportionate to its size — and whose Black population is at once a farm-county inheritance and a university community. The state tables place Macon County among Alabama's Blackest counties in every census of the modern era, and Tuskegee's position within it is distinctive even there: it is the rare Black Belt seat whose economy and identity are organized around an institution of higher learning rather than a courthouse square and a farm trade alone. That shows in the details of the profile — the bachelor's share lifted by the university, the population's college-age bulge, the steady traffic of alumni and visitors through a city of 9,400. It also shows in the community's self-understanding: Tuskegee's Black residents are, to an unusual degree, the citizens of a place that Black America regards as its own landmark, and the census's 84 percent is simply the count of the people who keep it.
