Homeownership: The National Plate

Homeownership is the tenure line that divides the housing map, and this plate draws it for Black America at national scale. In recent federal readings — the Census Bureau's housing surveys and the American Community Survey — the Black homeownership rate stands at about forty-four to forty-six percent of Black households, against about sixty-five to sixty-six percent for households nationally. Behind the single rate sits a state pattern that surprises readers who know only the population map: the highest Black ownership rates are recorded not in the wealthy coastal states but in the deep South, and the lowest in some of the northern states whose Black populations are smallest. The thematic housing plates develop the full picture; this plate fixes its national frame.

The state pattern

Mississippi records the highest Black homeownership rate in the nation in recent survey readings, in the mid-to-high fifties percent, with South Carolina and Alabama close behind — states where land and housing costs are comparatively low, where Black settlement is old and often rural, and where ownership has passed within families across generations. The southern tier generally out-owns the nation on this measure. At the pattern's other end, North Dakota and Minnesota read lowest, and the expensive coastal states — California, New York, Hawaii — record Black ownership in the thirties despite their high incomes, because the price of entry governs the rate more than the income of the residents does. Washington, D.C., despite its high Black incomes, reads low for the same reason. The state income plates and this tenure map, read together, teach the atlas's recurring lesson: the measures do not move together, and each must be mapped on its own.

The household frame

The rate is computed over households, and the Black household frame is worth stating plainly, because every housing figure in the atlas sits on it. The nation holds roughly fifteen to sixteen million Black households. A majority of Black adults are householders or partners of householders; multigenerational living — grandparents, adult children, extended kin under one roof — runs at higher rates than the national pattern, and the American Community Survey's household tables, drawn on in the thematic plates, count those arrangements directly. Rental tenure, the complement of the ownership rate, concentrates in the large metros and carries the cost-burden measures — the share of households paying more than thirty percent of income for housing — that the housing plates map alongside tenure, so that owning and renting are each described in full rather than one standing as the other's shadow.

How to read a tenure plate

Tenure figures come from two instruments that do not agree to the decimal: the Current Population Survey's housing series, which produces the headline national rate, and the American Community Survey, which produces the state and metro grain. The plates name the instrument on every table and never splice the two in a single comparison. Rates also move with the age of the householder population — ownership climbs steeply with age in every group — and the thematic plates carry the age-banded readings so that a young population is not misread as a failing one. What the tenure map shows, within those stated cautions, is one of the atlas's most instructive inversions: the deepest Black geographies by share are also, on this measure, the most rooted by ownership, and the plates that follow let that fact sit beside the income and health maps of the same counties, where the story it tells becomes more complicated and more true.

Tenure inside the atlas

Tenure returns in the thematic housing plates in its full federal detail: ownership by age of householder, where the Black rate climbs from the teens among the youngest householders to the seventies among the oldest; ownership by household type; the renter cost-burden map; and the value map, which records what owned homes are worth — a measure on which the same deep-South states that lead in ownership rate read lowest, since a house's price and a household's rootedness are different facts. The metro plates carry tenure for the forty largest metros, where several of the historically deepest Black homeownership markets in the country — Memphis, Birmingham, Jackson — sit inside the same metros that lead the share rankings. And the gazetteer lets a reader begin from a place and see its tenure beside its income, so the two are never read in isolation. The national plate has done its work if the reader keeps the inversion in view: on the tenure measure, the map of Black America is strongest exactly where it is oldest, and thinnest where prices, not people, keep the door closed.
