The Alabama Education

Education turn on attainment — the credentials a population carries. This places Black Alabama in the national education geography: the benchmarks below are the published national attainment figures for Black adults, and Alabama's distribution figures — 1.4 million Black residents, 26 percent of the state — give the scale of the community those national patterns describe within the state's borders.

The National Pattern

Educational attainment — the highest level of schooling a person has completed — is among the most consequential statistics in American demographic data, because it predicts earnings, employment, health, and wealth more reliably than almost any other single measure. For Black adults aged 25 and over, the Census Bureau's surveys record a population that has reached near-parity in high school completion while remaining well behind national rates in college completion: about 89 percent hold a high school diploma or higher, and about 25 percent — one in four — hold a bachelor's degree or higher. This article reports the attainment data, the comparisons it supports, and the trend behind it.

Attainment is measured in steps. At the first step, high school completion, Black adults stand at about 89 percent, close to the national figure of about 90 percent — a convergence that is historically recent and remarkable, given that as late as the mid-twentieth century a majority of Black adults had not completed high school. At the second step, some college or an associate degree, roughly another quarter of Black adults stand. At the third step, the bachelor's degree, the Black figure of about 25 percent separates from the national figure of about 36 percent and the non-Hispanic White figure of about 39 percent. At the top step, advanced degrees — master's, professional, and doctoral — about one in ten Black adults holds one, against roughly one in seven adults nationally.

Placed alongside the other groups the Census Bureau reports, the bachelor's-degree ordering reads: Asian adults at roughly 57 percent, non-Hispanic White adults at 39 percent, all adults at 36 percent, Black adults at 25 percent, and Hispanic adults at about 21 percent. Two features of this ordering matter for interpretation. The Black rate is not the lowest, and the distance between the Black rate and the national rate — about eleven points — is concentrated entirely at the college steps, not at high school completion. Analysts accordingly describe the Black attainment gap as a college completion gap, not a general schooling gap: the divergence happens between enrollment and graduation, a distinction the enrollment and graduation articles in this section take up separately.

Reading This

How to read this: the state rows are the published census figures for Alabama; the benchmark rows are the published national attainment record, labeled as benchmarks. Attainment is a stock built over decades, so the national pattern changes slowly — and the size of the state's Black adult population is what turns that pattern into local classrooms, colleges, and workforces.

The Population Behind the

For Alabama, the national attainment figures are context; the state's own distribution, tabled below, is the subject. Alabama holds about 1.4 million Black residents — Black alone or in combination — more than one in four of its people, with Black alone at about 1.3 million, or 26 percent. The comparison the invites is therefore two-sided: the national ladder on one side, Alabama's attainment on the other.

The attainment ladder reads locally in Alabama: the state rows below give the population whose schooling this national pattern frames. The Black Belt arcs across Alabama's midsection from the Georgia line to Mississippi: a band of chalk-soil prairie counties — Wilcox, Lowndes, Dallas (whose seat is Selma), Greene, Sumter, Marengo, Hale, Perry, and their neighbors — where enslaved majorities worked the cotton economy and where Black majorities, in several counties exceeding 70 percent, persist today. That is the use of a benchmark in an atlas: not to rank the state, but to locate it.

Attainment is cumulative: every graduating class adds to it, and nothing subtracts except time. That is why education reward patience — the national Black attainment record has risen across every measured generation, and a state like Alabama, holding 1.4 million Black residents, holds its share of that rising stock in its own schools and campuses. The benchmarks are the levels against which any state reading is compared.

The record behind this of Alabama is published, not estimated by this atlas: National Center for Education Statistics; U.S. Census Bureau, American Community Survey educational attainment tables. Where the table carries a national benchmark row, the row is labeled with the basis it is published on, so the comparison it invites is an honest one — the same measure, the same basis, two geographies.
